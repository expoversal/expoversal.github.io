What's new in Evalia

Changes to Evalia, newest first, written for people following the product. Results come from public tender records; firms are not named. Evalia proposes; the evaluation panel decides every score.

14 November 2026

  • Terms a firm accepts by submitting are no longer checked as content. Solicitations set terms that bind every respondent simply by responding: bearing its own costs, keeping the offer valid, staying silent with staff during the selection, how to mark trade secrets. No submission can show that it meets them, yet Evalia's reading filed them as content to be shown, so on the sixth case 154 of 291 "not addressed" findings were such terms. The reading now sorts them into their own kind; they are listed once in the compliance record, with their pages, and not checked against each bidder. Anything a submission must contain to meet a term, such as a signed acknowledgement, is still checked. On the sixth case, re-reading the solicitation sorted 19 terms into the new kind, each of which reads correctly as a term accepted by submitting; six of the 14 requirements that all eleven firms had “missed” are among them, which removes 66 findings an evaluator would have had to clear. The other eight include the page limit and format rules, which a submission can show, so they stay.
  • Questions about the documents. An evaluator can now ask a question about the solicitation, its addenda or a submission, such as what bonding capacity is required or who a firm proposes as superintendent. Code picks the pages that share the question's words, a model answers from them, and code checks every quote on the page it cites: an answer is shown only with at least one verified quote, and quotes that fail are dropped and counted. The answer's wording is marked as an interpretation and the quotes as what the documents say. Price documents are not searched, Evalia's own findings and scores are never used, and every question, answered or not, is listed in the review record. Tested on fictional documents; not yet tried on a real tender.
  • Checks on a bidder's programme. Evalia can now read a programme exported from Primavera P6 (.xer) or Microsoft Project (.xml) and check its integrity by code: activities without logic, leads and lags, links other than finish-to-start, constraints that override logic, high and negative float, very long activities, dates on the wrong side of the data date, and the last finish against the required completion date. The thresholds are those commonly published for the DCMA 14-point assessment. Each result lists the activities concerned, and being outside a threshold is presented as a question to put to the bidder, not as non-compliance. No model is used. Tested on fictional exports built by hand; not yet tried on a real bidder's programme. For now it runs as a script, not from the application's screens.
  • Open contracting data, out and in. A closed evaluation can now be exported in the Open Contracting Data Standard, the format Chile and many other governments publish in: who tendered, and each bid's standing on the compliance decisions. Scores are not exported, since the standard has no place for them and whether to publish them is the owner's decision. The export needs the owner's own registered identifier prefix, which Evalia does not invent, and it is written for a person to check and publish; Evalia publishes nothing. In the other direction, Evalia reads a published OCDS file and lists each tender's buyer, tenderers, dates and documents with their addresses, as a starting point for a new procurement. Tested on fictional data; not yet tried on a real portal's file. For now it runs as a script.

13 November 2026

  • Model fees corrected. Evalia's price table held Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $3 and $15 per million input and output tokens; Anthropic publishes $2 and $10. Every fee Evalia reported was a half higher than it should have been, and the application's cost display also left out the prompt cache. Both are fixed, with one price table for the application and every script. Recomputed from the stored records, the six public evaluations cost about $26 in model fees in all, not $34, and that now includes work the old figure left out. No finding about agreement with panels changes.

12 November 2026

  • Fill the comparison from the panel's spreadsheet. When an owner compares Evalia with an earlier evaluation, it can now choose the panel's own Excel or CSV score sheet instead of typing the scores. Code finds the headings, matches the columns to the criteria or to a total and the rows to the bidders, and fills the form; it lists what it could not match, and the person checks every figure before comparing. No model is used, and nothing is recorded until the person compares.
  • The sixth case, completed. Stage two, where the five shortlisted firms gave technical proposals and presentations, was scored and compared: with four firms' material in the records and a presentation Evalia did not see, it is reported, not judged. The compliance check on all eleven statements raised two real points, both on naming subcontractors, and showed that terms a firm accepts by submitting, such as bearing its own costs, are being checked as content to be shown. Sorting the two is the next improvement to the check. Model fees for both: about $5.
  • Arabic screens, as a draft. Evalia's screens can now be shown in Arabic, right to left, chosen under Settings, for pilots in the Gulf. Documents, quotes and records keep their own language. The translation was made without a native speaker and is marked as a draft: it is to be reviewed by one before any client relies on it, as with the Spanish screens. Some text built by the program from pieces still appears in English.
  • One obligation, checked once. A solicitation often asks for a form in its checklist and again on the form itself, and Evalia's reading listed both, so a person had to clear the same finding twice. Entries that name the same form or attachment and share most of their words are now checked as one, keeping the stricter kind and the pages where the solicitation repeats it. Entries that name the same form for different things are kept apart. On the two latest real cases, this merges three pairs and two pairs, each checked by eye to be the same obligation. No model is involved.
  • Why the sixth case disagreed. The evaluators' narratives show that the committee also scored what it knew of firms from its own projects, which the solicitation allowed and which is not in any submission. One evaluator noted that a firm lacked the medical examiner experience the solicitation favoured and scored it well on its county record. Evalia scores only what the submissions contain, so part of the gap is structural rather than an error on either side.
  • A pilot pack for a closed tender, in English and Spanish, as a draft awaiting sign-off: what a firm provides and gets, how its documents stay on its own computer, and an outline of the agreement.

11 November 2026

  • A sixth real evaluation, the largest since the first week. Evalia scored the eleven statements of qualifications for a Florida county's construction manager selection, with the measures fixed before the run and its scores recorded before the committee's sheets were read. Rank correlation with the committee: 0.42, inconclusive against the threshold fixed in advance. Evalia put the committee's first two firms at the top, in reverse order; its agreement with each evaluator (−0.01 to 0.72) lay within the evaluators' agreement with one another (−0.20 to 0.75). The largest difference was experience with public medical facilities, which the solicitation said would count in a firm's favour. A page in one statement stopped the reader because its page box did not start at zero; that was fixed. Model fees were about $4.37.
  • A fifth real evaluation. Evalia was run on a Florida county's selection of a construction manager for a fire station: the solicitation, two addenda, the four shortlisted statements of qualifications and the committee's score sheets, all public. The measures were fixed before the run, and Evalia's scores were recorded before anyone read the committee's sheets. With four firms, the ranking comparison is reported but not judged. Of the two firms the committee interviewed, Evalia's written scores put the eventual winner ahead; the committee's written scores put the other firm ahead by one point, and reversed the order after the interviews. Evalia again scored more strictly than the evaluators. Model fees were about $5.60.
  • Addenda now prevail in the compliance check. The fifth case showed that six of nine "contradicted" findings were against requirements an addendum had removed or relaxed. Evalia now matches each addendum change to the requirements it alters: a requirement an addendum removed is no longer checked, an option an addendum allows is not treated as a requirement, and a changed requirement is checked as it now stands, with the addendum named beside it. Nothing is deleted from the reading. The fix passes its tests on a fictional addendum. Rerun on the real case: the contradicted findings fell from nine to six, none of the six wrong ones recurred, and the six that remain are for a person to judge.

10 November 2026

  • Compare with an earlier evaluation. An owner can now try Evalia on a procurement it has already evaluated and see the two side by side. Once the evaluation in Evalia is closed, and not before, a person enters the earlier panel's scores or totals, the issues it recorded and its hours, naming the document they come from. Code, not a model, compares the rankings, the scores by criterion, the issues each side recorded, the time and the model fees, against thresholds fixed in advance, and writes a document in which each claim is labelled. Nothing changes either evaluation, and the document says that one procurement establishes no accuracy rate. On the fictional procurement, with three bidders, it reported the ranking as not judged (too few bidders) and one of the earlier evaluation's two issues as found by both.

9 November 2026

  • Feature list without dates. The list of features in the prototype no longer shows the date each was built. The changelog keeps the history; the list now reads as what Evalia does.

8 November 2026

  • The work done, at the top of the prototype. The prototype now opens on what has been built: the number of features, areas, merged changes and automated tests, counted from the repository when the page is built, and the eleven areas with one line each. The full list follows as the first step, area by area, each feature with its date. It states that none has yet been used on a live tender.
  • Pre-check decisions shown in the review. An item in the main review now shows the pre-check decision on the same point for the same bidder, with who made it, when and why, so the evaluator builds on it instead of starting again. It is a person's record, not Evalia's view, so it is shown before the evaluator's own decision; it decides nothing, and a blind re-check hides it.
  • One log for every exchange with bidders. Clarifications on lines a bidder added to its bill now go through the same log as all others: the request uses the wording that cannot invite a change of price, and the log keeps when it was sent, the reply and the outcome. The levelling workbook and the price record list them. Replies recorded the earlier way remain readable.
  • Pass/fail thresholds in the pre-check. A procurement can now list the thresholds its solicitation states, such as a minimum turnover or a maximum safety rate, with the words that name each figure. Code reads the figure from each submission, with the passage and page it came from, and marks it passed, flagged or not found; the evaluator decides. On the fictional procurement it read all three bidders' safety rates and flagged the one above the limit.
  • Exclusions labelled by what they put at risk. On the price card the evaluator can label each exclusion or qualification a bidder states, for example life safety, structural, long-lead item or interface with other packages. Evalia proposes no label. The labels appear in the levelling workbook, and the price record counts them by bidder.
  • Scanned pages read on the evaluator's computer (optional). A procurement can now have its scanned pages read by a reader installed on the evaluator's computer, instead of sending their images to the AI provider. Pages read with low confidence are flagged. A quote on such a page is never shown as found word for word, because a machine reading can be wrong: on a fictional test page it read 0.95 as O.95. If the reader is not installed, Evalia refuses rather than sending the pages elsewhere. The reader's libraries are pinned and checked like the rest; installing it is optional.
  • Evalia in Spanish. The two screens can now be shown in Spanish, chosen under Settings on each computer, with a short guide in Spanish to the whole evaluation. Text from documents, quotes and names is never translated; a phrase not yet translated stays in English. The records Evalia writes remain in English for now.
  • The prototype rebuilt on today's fictional documents. The published prototype had been built before one fictional bidder's tender was revised on 7 November. Rebuilt, it shows that bidder's higher price, its tender security now short of the amount required and its changed price schedule. The site's links and notices are now kept in the repository, so that a rebuild keeps them.
  • Sentences shared between bidders. The pre-check now lists any sentence of 12 words or more that appears word for word in two bidders' submissions, leaving out wording taken from the solicitation and, with three bidders or more, wording every bidder shares. On the fictional procurement the first version flagged every bidder over standard form answers; with these two rules one standard answer remains, which the evaluator can mark as no concern. Shared text is an indicator for the integrity process, never a finding.
  • Agreement figures for the chair. The moderation pack, which opens only once every evaluator has returned, now shows for each criterion how far the evaluators' scores spread across bidders, and the range of rank agreement between evaluators beside Evalia's agreement with each of them. A wide spread on one criterion usually means its scoring guidance is read differently; neither figure measures any evaluator's performance.
  • Lines a bidder did not price, with reference figures. For each line a bidder left out, left blank or marked "included", the price card now shows what the other bidders and the estimate priced it at, at the tender quantity, computed by code. The evaluator can copy a figure into a comparison adjustment and must still give the reason. Evalia proposes no adjustment, and the tendered price never changes.
  • A bid tabulation to publish. The price card can now write a tabulation in the form many public owners publish: each bidder's total as read and as recomputed by code, the difference and the number of findings, ranked on the totals as read, as a CSV file and a short document. It states whether the price evaluation is closed and that a difference is a finding to decide, not a corrected price. Publishing it remains the owner's decision.
  • A features page in the prototype. The prototype now opens with a section that lists every feature built since the project started on 29 October, 66 in 11 groups, each with the date it was built, from evidence checked by code to the results measured on published public evaluations. It says plainly that each is built and tested on fictional and published documents and that none has yet been used on a live tender.
  • A responsiveness pre-check, and what changed between versions of the solicitation. Before anyone evaluates, Evalia now checks each submission by code: documents and pages, the page limit, required documents by name, addenda acknowledged by number in five languages, and the validity period. The evaluator decides every check in a review of its own; anything code cannot find is shown as "not found by code", never as non-compliance. Two versions of the solicitation can be compared sentence by sentence, each change with its page.
  • A clarification log from finding to outcome. A finding decided as "Clarification sought" now gives a request drafted by code from its verified quotes. The evaluator records when it was sent, the reply and when it arrived, and the outcome with a reason; every step is kept with its time. A reply that may offer a price change is flagged. A procurement can also ask Evalia to flag prices far below the others or the estimate: Evalia then drafts the request for an explanation that the law requires before any rejection, and never proposes the rejection itself.
  • The owner's estimate, quantity corrections and the ranking they produce. A procurement can now state the owner's estimate and the thresholds at which a price calls for analysis, including the Florida Department of Transportation's published ones. The price card shows each price against the estimate. An evaluator who finds a wrong tender quantity records the correction and its reason; Evalia applies it to every bidder by code and shows whether the lowest bidder changes. Rate indicators now ask for the quantity to be checked first. Thresholds open an analysis; Evalia never proposes a rejection.
  • Price points with the working shown. A procurement can now state the price-scoring formula its solicitation publishes, from five common forms. Evalia applies it by code to each bidder's evaluated price and shows the arithmetic, line by line, on the price card and in the price record. Evalia never chooses a formula.
  • Numbers written the local way. A procurement whose documents write 1.234,56 can now say so, and Evalia reads its prices that way. Under either convention, a number that fits neither reading is left unread instead of being read wrongly: before this change, "1,5" could be read as 15. Price columns headed in Spanish are recognised.
  • Arabic documents. Arabic text read from a PDF used to come out reversed and in joined letter forms, so the AI read it scrambled and no Arabic quote could be found on its page. Evalia now puts right-to-left lines and tables back in reading order. It also compares Arabic quotes without the optional vowel marks, which a writer may include or leave out without changing a word. Tested on a fictional Arabic page; no real Arabic tender has been run.
  • Tests for scoring schemes in stages. The fix of 6 November for solicitations that score a written stage and an interview stage separately is now covered by tests.
  • Exactly the libraries reviewed. Every installation now receives exactly the versions of Evalia's libraries that were checked, each matched against its published fingerprint; the installer refuses any file that does not match. Each release package also comes with its own fingerprint, so that an owner's IT staff can confirm the copy they received.
  • A fictional tender in Spanish. A third fictional tender, a community centre in an invented Chilean town, priced the Chilean way, rehearses a Latin American test without real data. The price checks find exactly the faults planted in it, and read with the wrong number convention its figures are left unread rather than misread.
  • Accessibility re-checked on the new screens. The settings page with the self-hosted model address, and a procurement sealed as a pilot with its award form, were scanned in both themes with no violations; the award form can be used from the keyboard alone. The repository's copy of the conformance report records this; the copy on the website is unchanged.
  • Draft system card for an owner's own model. The description of the self-hosted model route now states what was tested on 8 November and what was not. It is a draft awaiting sign-off.
  • Local server closed to other websites. A security check found that a web page open in the evaluator's browser could send requests to Evalia's local server, for example to create a procurement or change a setting. Evalia now refuses any request that does not come from its own pages on the same computer. The check also found no known vulnerability in the libraries Evalia uses; its results are in a new security review, a draft not yet signed off.
  • Sealed pilots. A procurement run as a pilot alongside a live evaluation can now keep Evalia's documents sealed until a person records that the award has been decided. Until then the application neither shows nor exports any of them, so nothing from Evalia can reach the award or be handed out before it. Recording the award, with its date and reference, opens them and is logged.
  • An owner's own model. Evalia can now use a model that the owner runs in its own country or on its own servers, entered under Settings. Each procurement states where its documents may be processed, and Evalia refuses to send them anywhere else. A trial on the fictional procurement with a small open model showed the route working end to end and the refusal enforced. That model read the tender poorly, which shows that a self-hosted model must itself be tested before any owner relies on it.
  • Measures fixed before the next tests. Two plans written before any data is seen: one for every pilot on a live procurement, setting what is compared with the panel and what counts as agreement, and one for a test on a public Chilean tender in Spanish, which also checks whether Evalia's readings survive a second language.
  • A second fictional tender, to check Evalia is not tuned to one example. A design-and-build water pumping station, in metric units and euros, with one lot, four bidders, a 70/30 quality-price award and differently headed price columns. Each bidder carries known faults: a wrong extension, an altered quantity, a reduced provisional sum, an omitted line, a rate marked "Included" and a line added outside the schedule. The price checks find exactly those faults and nothing else, and a fault-free bidder produces no findings. The tender is fictional and stamped as such on every page.
  • Training without scores, and a guide to the first measurement round. A fictional procurement that has been checked but not scored can now be turned into a training copy, with errors planted in the compliance findings only. A new guide explains how to run the first round with five to eight evaluators: one copy each, the key kept away from them, the questionnaires afterwards, and how to report the result with its uncertainty. No round has been run yet.
  • Project handoff note updated. The note that lets a collaborator pick up the work now describes the application as it stands, the decisions taken and the actions still open.

7 November 2026

  • Review list headings no longer overlap. In the review screen, the headings that group findings by requirement stacked on top of one another when the list was scrolled. They now scroll with their group.
  • A tour of the application. The site now shows Evalia's own screens on a fictional procurement, from uploading the documents to the debrief letters, with a two-and-a-half-minute video of how it works. The tender is fictional, its findings were read by a live AI model, and the decisions shown were recorded for the demonstration. The earlier prototype stays online.
  • Training procurements, to measure what evaluators catch. A training copy of a fictional procurement can now be made with some of Evalia's proposals altered on purpose and a key kept apart. When the evaluator closes it, Evalia reports how many of the planted errors were caught. It refuses any tender not stamped as fictional, so that errors are never planted in a real evaluation, and it says on every screen that it is training. No measurement round has been run yet.
  • The review learns within one procurement only. When an evaluator has changed one in five or more of Evalia's readings of one kind, at least five decided, the rest of that kind are sent to be decided one by one. Nothing learnt in one procurement is carried to another.
  • The fictional example now has a line added outside the bill. One fictional bidder adds an item to its priced schedule, so that the review of added lines can be tried on the example. Another bidder meets every gate and can be awarded the contract.
  • Legal citations checked against the texts. The procurement rules cited by Evalia's research were checked against primary sources. Most were confirmed; the description of US architect-engineer selection and one design-build citation were corrected. The US debriefing letters now cite the new section numbers used by agencies applying the 2025-26 overhaul of the Federal Acquisition Regulation. Their drafting notes now say which items the officer must add, including unit prices and past performance.
  • A closed evaluation now reads as closed, whoever opens it. In the Windows test, the evaluator's name in Settings had changed after the evaluation was closed, so the review counted none of the 129 decisions as made and showed them all as pending. A closed review now shows the decisions in the closed record and says plainly that nothing is pending. An open review that holds decisions under another name now says so, instead of silently ignoring them.
  • One look on every computer, and clearer steps. Evalia now carries its own open typeface, Public Sans, so that it looks the same everywhere and needs no network, and both screens share one set of colours and styles. Each procurement page opens with a list of its steps and the status of each, in words. Patterns from the UK and US government design systems were adapted, without their branding: a list to check before closing an evaluation, a banner for a legal hold or a step completed, and an error summary on the settings page. In the review, accepting a finding faster than its quoted evidence can be read now brings a private note offering to undo it.
  • A faster, clearer review that still asks for judgement. Research on expert review tools and on over-reliance on AI led to a redesign of the review screen. Each finding now opens on the quoted passage, in larger type, with whether it was found on its page said in words; Evalia's reasons are folded beneath. Every decision has one key, so agreeing with Evalia is no quicker than disagreeing, and each decision can be undone, with both kept in the record. The next pages load ahead, so moving on is immediate. After each decision Evalia says what it changed, such as a requirement now settled for every bidder, and the top bar counts requirements settled rather than clicks. A summary at closing shows the evaluator's own judgement: decisions made before Evalia's reading, amendments with reasons, blind checks. It shows no agreement rate or speed. Tested on the fictional procurement; whether it changes speed or accuracy has not been measured.
  • Colleagues' scores, only after one's own are locked. On a panel, the moderator can share every evaluator's final scores and reasons once all have returned; each evaluator can open them only after making their own return, ready for the moderation meeting.
  • Accessibility checked against WCAG 2.2 AA. An automated scan found missing names on form fields, a contrast failure and gaps in announcements; all were fixed, and the scan now reports no violations on any screen, in light and dark themes. Keyboard use, zoom to 200 per cent and narrow screens were checked too. A draft accessibility conformance report, on the template public buyers ask for, records this; it states that no test with a screen reader, or by users with disabilities, has yet been done.
  • Several evaluators, by role, who never see each other's work. A moderator, who does not evaluate, can now give each role (for example technical, contract or price) an evaluator and the criteria it scores. Each evaluator receives a package holding only their role's documents and Evalia's proposals, reviews on their own computer and returns a locked file. The moderation pack stays closed until every evaluator who scores has returned, so that no score is seen before all are in. Prices can be kept sealed until the quality evaluation is returned, kept from quality evaluators altogether, or shown to them as a recorded decision with its basis. Tested with four workspaces standing in for four computers; not yet tried by a real panel.
  • Less rubber-stamping in the review. Research on automation bias and review fatigue, from e-discovery, audit, medical second reading and procurement guidance, led to six changes. Gates are now decided by the evaluator before Evalia's reading is shown, as scores already were. Findings that need judgement come first. Accepting several findings together needs each to be opened and ticked. One in five of the findings accepted together is then drawn at random and decided again blind; a check that disagrees reopens the others. Closing also waits for a finding on every requirement and bidder, and the page shows how long decisions took and whether acceptance rose as the review went on. Tested on the fictional procurement; whether these changes reduce errors has not been measured.
  • Lines that bidders add to the bill, reviewed properly. Lines a bidder adds outside the schedule issued are listed apart, with the nearest schedule item and whether the amount counts in the total. The evaluator chooses what each line is and how to deal with it, and Evalia drafts a clarification that asks the bidder to explain the line without changing its price; a reply that offers to re-price is flagged. Adjustments for comparison change only the evaluated price; the tendered price is always kept and shown. Tested on synthetic bills.
  • A declaration of interests before review. Each evaluator now declares any conflict of interest with the bidders before any submission is shown in the review. A declared conflict stops that evaluator's review, and the declaration goes into the record.
  • The review now shows what is left, and moves on. A test on Windows found that an evaluator could decide every finding and still be refused the close, with nothing on screen to say why. Fourteen findings that Evalia read as "outside the submission" (for example, whether a tender arrived on time) had been accepted, which records no compliance decision. After each further click the screen stayed on the same finding, so one finding was decided 25 times while the others waited. Now:
  • The fictional example now includes prices. The example procurement that comes with the installer now has a price schedule and each bidder's priced schedule as spreadsheets, with the same figures as their tender PDFs. On it, the price check finds the errors the example was made with: in one bidder's schedule, an item extended wrongly, so that its total does not add up, and a provisional sum cut from $250,000 to $150,000. It finds nothing in the other two.
  • The data-handling statement now covers retention, legal hold and prices. It now says three things. A client can record how long each procurement is kept and place it on legal hold, and Evalia never deletes anything. In the price stage, the text of bidders' price documents goes to the AI provider so that it can list exclusions, while all price arithmetic stays on the client's computer. 6 November 2026

  • Evidence cited, not copied: built, not yet measured. A new scoring option, `two-step-cited`, lists the evidence for each criterion through the AI provider's citation feature. Evalia passes each submission as numbered sentences of known pages, and the AI cites them instead of copying quotes. Every quote is then the submission's own sentence, with its page known, and Evalia still checks it. On one earlier real case, about a fifth of quotes cited a page other than the one they were on, and this aims to remove that. It is tested only with a stand-in for the provider. The test plan and its decision rule were written before any real run; the run on two public cases will cost about $15. The default scoring method is unchanged.
  • Prices checked, after the technical evaluation and apart from it. Bidders' price documents can now be added to a procurement, in PDF, Excel or CSV. They stay sealed until the technical evaluation is closed, as in a two-envelope procurement. Evalia then checks each priced bill by code against the schedule the client issued: extensions, totals, items left out, blank or marked "included", and quantities, units and provisional sums changed. The AI lists every exclusion and qualification, and code checks each one's quote on its page. The evaluator decides every item in a separate price review with its own close. Evalia writes a levelling workbook that puts the bids side by side, every figure traced to its file and cell. On synthetic priced bills with seven errors put in on purpose, Evalia found all seven, and raised nothing that had not been put in. Evalia proposes no price adjustment.
  • Debrief letters, drafted for every bidder. Once an evaluation is closed and the award decided, Evalia drafts a Word letter for each bidder, as a US debriefing or a UK assessment summary. Each letter gives the bidder its own scores, reasons and compliance decisions. It gives only what the rules allow about the winner: in the US, its name, total and price, with no point-by-point comparison; in the UK, also its score and explanation on each criterion, as the regulations require. A check by code refuses to write any letter that would name another bidder, and withholds any explanation that does. No AI drafts the letters, so every bidder's letter is built the same way. The officer enters the award, then edits, signs and sends the letters. Tested on a fictional procurement.
  • Retention dates and legal hold. For each procurement, the client can now record how long it is kept, and the schedule or rule the date comes from. Evalia proposes no date. When a protest, claim or records request arrives, the client places the procurement on legal hold, with a reason. Evalia then refuses every change to it, while everything can still be read and downloaded. Evalia never deletes anything. A procurement past its date and not on hold is marked due for disposal, and the client disposes of it under its own records process. Every date and hold is logged with the person and the time, and the evaluation record states those in force when it was written. Tested on fictional procurements.
  • Documents brought up to date. The project's front page, architecture, scope and running guide now describe Evalia as it is: a local application that an evaluator installs and uses unaided, tested on four real public evaluations, with milestone M4 partly met and the first client pilot not yet started.
  • One requirement, every bidder, side by side. Reviewing findings one at a time does not scale: three bidders on a fictional procurement already gave 129. The evaluator can now open one requirement and see every bidder's finding beside its page. Findings read as addressed, with the quoted passage found word for word on its page, can be accepted together in one action with one reason. Any finding read differently, or whose passage was not confirmed, is still decided on its own. The record shows which findings were accepted together.
  • Removal checked on Windows, and made safe to repeat. On the first try the uninstaller removed the key, the shortcuts and the settings but not the program, because Windows would not delete a folder in use. It now refuses to start while Evalia is running, before removing anything, and runs from outside Evalia's folder. The PDF printer that writes the evaluation record was also confirmed on Windows.
  • First installation on Windows, by a person following the guide. If the solicitation is read again and Evalia reads exactly the same criteria, points and requirements, the evaluator's confirmation stays in force; if anything differs, the criteria must be confirmed again before any score is proposed.   • Closing an evaluation, with a complete record. The evaluator can now review one bidder at a time or one criterion across all bidders, with the items in a random order to guard against order effects and rubber-stamping. For each requirement the decision is one of four: complies, does not comply, minor irregularity waived, or clarification sought. Accepting a finding that a submission does not comply needs a reason. Evalia refuses to close the evaluation while any proposal is undecided, or while bidders in the same position were decided differently without a reason for each decision. Closing writes the compliance record, a record of every decision with its reason and time, the evaluator's scoresheet for the moderation meeting, the audit file, the evaluation record and the statement on the use of AI; after that, no decision can be changed. On a fictional procurement with 162 proposals, closing was refused until every one was decided, the record listed all 162, and an independent check of the audit file found no differences.

  • From an empty folder to findings, without the command line. Evalia now runs as a page in the evaluator's browser, on their own computer. The evaluator creates a procurement, adds the PDFs and says which is the solicitation and which bidder sent each submission. Nothing is read until that register is confirmed. Evalia then reads the criteria and their points, with the checks on the scoring scheme beside them. The evaluator corrects or confirms them, and no score is proposed before that. Checking and scoring start from buttons, with progress and cost shown, and every run is recorded. On a fictional procurement with three bidders, the whole sequence took under four minutes of AI work and cost $0.71, and all 162 findings opened at their pages for review.

  • A review screen for the evaluator. Evalia now opens a case on the evaluator's own computer, in the browser, and shows each proposed score or finding beside the page it cites, with the quoted passage highlighted. For a scored criterion the evaluator reads the submission and gives their own score first; only then does Evalia show its proposal, its reasons and the passages it relied on. The evaluator accepts, amends or rejects each one, and Evalia refuses any departure from its proposal, or from the evaluator's own first score, without a reason. Every decision is kept with the evaluator, the time and the seconds spent on it, and earlier decisions are never overwritten. On a real 54-score evaluation, all 109 cited pages opened, the whole quote was highlighted on 101 and its opening words on five more, and every decision survived a restart. Evalia now records which stage each criterion belongs to, such as written submissions or interviews, and checks each stage's points separately, so a two-stage solicitation is no longer flagged in error. On a 13-firm construction-manager selection, Evalia's ranking agreed with the panel's consensus at 0.725, within the range of the evaluators' agreement with each other, with the same first two firms in the same order and the same last.   • Improvements measured on real evaluations. Splitting scoring into two steps (evidence first, checked by code, then scores) left no quote unfound on its page, but made scores vary more between runs and cost about 45 per cent more, so the single-step method stays the default. Caching cut the cost of those runs by 18 per cent. Taking the median of three runs reproduced its earlier result at no cost. A cheaper model read 50 test scans almost as well but misread one contract value, so the current model stays for scanned pages.
  • A safeguard against empty answers. The current AI model thinks before it answers, and on one long submission it used its whole allowance thinking and returned nothing, which was scored as zero. Evalia now refuses any answer cut off at its limit and asks again, and the allowance is three times larger.

A procurement can now use Evalia without any proposed scores: Evalia reads each submission against the solicitation's requirements, checks the arithmetic, and gives the panel a compliance matrix to decide, with the mandatory requirements it read as not met listed first. Suited to low-bid work and to clients not ready for AI-proposed scores.
  • Checks that hold up under protest. Code now checks the solicitation's own scheme (points against the stated total, percentage weights adding to 100, missing or duplicated criteria), that every score is on a published criterion within its range, and that bidders in the same position are treated alike: where they are decided differently, the record cannot be closed until each decision has a reason. Tested on fictional documents; not yet run on the real solicitations.

Tested on one real evaluation, three runs made the proposals steadier, with differences between runs about a third as large, but did not bring them closer to the panel's scores. Three runs cost about three times as much. The choice is recorded in every result and in the report.

In two-step scoring the AI first lists the evidence for each criterion, with quotes; code checks every quote against its page; the AI then scores from the checked evidence alone. The independent check of the audit file covers both steps. Tested on fictional documents; its effect on real evaluations has not yet been measured.
